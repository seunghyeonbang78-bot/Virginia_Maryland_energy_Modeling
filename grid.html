How the grid works

Supply has to follow demand.

Electricity use changes every hour. Different resources respond in different ways, so the grid combines steady generation, variable renewables, flexible plants, storage, imports, and demand response.

01 — Core rule

Generation ≈ demand, continuously.

Storage exists, but the grid still has to balance production and consumption nearly in real time.

Demand = Nuclear + Natural Gas + Solar + Wind + Hydro + Coal + Other + Imports + Storage Discharge − Storage Charging

02 — Resource roles

Why each source behaves differently.

⚛

Nuclear

High capital cost, low variable cost, and usually operated at high output for long periods. Good for steady generation.

steady / baseload
🔥

Natural Gas

Combined-cycle plants can run for long periods. Gas turbines can ramp quickly when demand rises or renewable output falls.

dispatchable
☀

Solar

Low fuel cost but variable. Strongest around midday and unavailable at night.

variable
≈

Wind

Output follows wind conditions. Useful when available, but not scheduled like a gas plant.

variable
💧

Hydro

Reservoir hydro can often be dispatched more flexibly than wind and solar, within water and environmental limits.

controllable
↕

Pumped Storage

Uses electricity to pump water uphill, storing gravitational potential energy that can later be converted back to electricity.

storage

03 — Why Virginia and Maryland differ

The same balancing rule produces different outcomes.

Virginia

Fast-growing demand

Virginia's electricity system is heavily affected by Northern Virginia data-center growth. That can keep natural gas, imports, transmission expansion, and storage important even while renewables grow.

Maryland

Nuclear + imports

Maryland starts with a much larger nuclear share and less in-state generation overall. Its long-term mix depends strongly on Calvert Cliffs, renewable build-out, and PJM imports.
